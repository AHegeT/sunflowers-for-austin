# Cleaned Transcript

### Speaker 1

Let's talk about the compakin process and how they are created.

  

Basically, a compakin needs three things: an egg, a partner, and a disk or core. The partner is a human whose biological footprint binds to the compakin. They care for the egg during its developmental process, shaping the compakin's personality and abilities to a certain extent.

  

Then there is the egg, which can be manufactured. It is a shell containing nutrients and a general developmental blueprint.

  

Finally, there is the digital core—also called the imaginal disk. This is the digital core of a human: it cannot be copied or reproduced. It is generated at the moment a human is uploaded to the Yosemite Continuum, serving as a blueprint of who that human is in the digital space. To use one for a compakin, the core must be willingly surrendered by the human; it cannot be forced. The core originates from a human giving up their digital identity.

  

The Yosemite Continuum is like tissue wrapped around bone. There are many different continuums, but we only have access to one, which has been named the Yosemite Continuum. It is not something humans built; rather, it is something humanity gained access to—not man-made, but man-reached.

  

The nurturing and biological binding form the compakin's personality. If the same egg and core were bound to a different human, the resulting compakin would turn out differently. Furthermore, when a compakin dies, you can recover the core and bind it into another egg to be reborn. If bound to that same human again, it will reflect that person's current version of themselves.

  

At the end of Book 1, Natalia's students all receive their compakins. Paloma receives her egg last because she lets others go first, and because she initially hesitates to use the core of a deceased loved one—her brother. All the kids bring a core from a family member, and she finally brings her brother's. While her egg is in the middle of development, the heist in Austin occurs, and her egg is stolen.

  

A snapshot of the egg is taken, capturing a version of the digital core, genetic material, and Paloma's developmental imprinting. The egg is used by Julia and the antagonists to mass-manufacture compakins.

  

Because a digital core cannot be replicated without decay, these manufactured versions are "lossy compakins." They cannot be used to enter the digital space; they are failures. They carry a fragment of Paloma's personality, but they decay rapidly, proving unstable, clingy, anxious, and short-lived.

  

The antagonist [Arcturus] figures out that the issue stems from the core. He attempts to fix this by binding compakins directly to the cores of the living customers who purchase them. This creates more stable compakins, but introduces a far worse problem: they are self-referential.

  

Because the core represents the human's identity blueprint in the digital continuum, the compakin views itself as the true human. Lacking the human's life memories but carrying their core identity, they become manipulative, secretive, and fiercely envious. It taps into the Freudian uncanny: encountering an exact duplicate of yourself, you either seek to subsume it or destroy it to reclaim your own uniqueness.

  

These are dubbed "autophagous compakins" (or "auto-compakins"). They view the living human as a lesser usurper. They sabotage the person's relationships, manipulate them, or lock them away inside the digital space so the compakin can pass as the human in the physical world.

  

We see this from the protagonist's viewpoint, but also through an in-universe short story about a girl who buys a compakin bonded to her own core without realizing it. She loved her previous compakin and hoped for another companion, but this creature subtly sabotages her life. It ruins her relationship with her boyfriend by sending messages masquerading as her. Eventually, the compakin dresses up in a wig and dress—an anthropomorphic, dinosaur-like creature pretending to be her—while some human victims are locked inside the digital space for eons as their compakins walk the earth.

  

The thematic arc parallels Julia and Paloma. The lossy compakins represent Paloma's inability to let go of her brother. Instead of destroying them, Paloma learns to show mercy and make the best of their limited lifespans. Ultimately, rather than keeping the snapshot alive, Paloma chooses to release her brother's core into the digital continuum.

  

Paloma acts as a foil to Julia: Paloma avoids acknowledging the past, while Julia obsesses over controlling it. Both must learn to let go.

  

Julia initially tries to eliminate the auto-compakins because they are dangerous and actively killing people. Eventually, Julia allows them to transition into the digital space to exist there on their own terms. This mirrors Julia's journey of letting go of control over her son and releasing her fixation on creating the "perfect child"—a flaw Arcturus repeated when engineering these creatures.

  

Instead of a healthy, symbiotic partnership where human and compakin protect each other, the auto-compakins create a parasitic dynamic where the human's digital core erodes away. By the end, the auto-compakins migrate into the digital space, which is recognized as their rightful home since they originated from human uploads.

  

Finally, Cody likely dies at the end of Book 2 protecting Natalia. It symbolizes the part of Natalia that broke when she betrayed Paloma, while echoing how Tulio died after his experience in Austin. Cody's digital core survives, however. It is placed into a new egg, and this time, Cody is raised jointly by both Paloma and Natalia.
