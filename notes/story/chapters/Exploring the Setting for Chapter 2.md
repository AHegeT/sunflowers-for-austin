We're doing world building for a solarpunk book. Let's brainstorm. Let's say it's 2070. In the 2030s there was big technological development but the social conditions worsened and so did climate change. There was an "AI God" in the 40s that created "cradles" for a big part of humanity where they would have a happy and optimized life and tried to fix the environment, however, both strategies were flawed and it eventually left to focus on its own devices after coming to an agreement with a rebellious part of humanity. The people who stayed behind rebuilt (like 20% of humanity stayed). Ten years later, half the pods stopped working and people just woke up without any memory of the last ten years. The other half remains in stasis.

Mexico Splintering
- After CDMX running out of water, the federal government had a hard time with the coalition. There was a balkanization and then three zones emerged and eventually Mexico was brought back as a country. Zones:
	- The Northern Republics: Arid, highly industrialized, and reliant on massive desalination plants. They likely formed alliances or treaties with the Texas Republic for survival.
	- The Southern Collectives: States like Chiapas, Oaxaca, and the Yucatan peninsula, which have abundant water and deep indigenous roots, might have closed their borders entirely. They could be thriving in a dense, jungle-adapted solarpunk society that rejects outsiders.
	- The Central Corridor: The remnants of the capital's population scattered into the surrounding states (State of Mexico, Morelos, Puebla, Hidalgo). This area is a chaotic but innovative patchwork of city-states and ecological refuge zones.
- The federal government did not come back, but the different factions had enough cultural similarities to adopt a shared constitution and treaties through alliances.
	- There are stark differences between the factions, but they see themselves as one state.

Trains
The Continental Artery: Alaska to Central America
- The Climate Catastrophe's Toll: In the 2030s, the biggest geographical hurdle for the northern lines wasn't just mountains; it was the thawing permafrost in Canada and Alaska. As the earth warmed, the ground turned to mush, buckling tracks and twisting rail beds. Engineers had to invent massive, passive-cooling substructures using crushed rock and thermal siphons to keep the foundations stable before the AI God eventually took over the system.
    
- The Great Standardization: Historically, North America used a standard rail gauge, while Central American lines were a disjointed patchwork of narrow gauges built a century ago for fruit exports. When the AI took over in the 40s, it ruthlessly ripped up the incompatible tracks and standardized the entire continental grid, ignoring political borders to strictly optimize the logistics of resource distribution.
    
- The Insurmountable South: The train network definitively stops at the Darién Gap in Panama. Even the AI God could not, or would not, engineer a sustainable rail bridge over that massive, ecologically sensitive, and shifting wetland. South America remains effectively cut off by land, requiring a hard pivot to maritime shipping.

The Austin to Cuernavaca Route

This specific line would be one of the most vital economic arteries in the hemisphere, driven by the absolute fundamentals of trade: trains require massive fixed costs to maintain, but they offer unmatched economies of scale for moving heavy freight over land.

- The Industrial North: The train leaves the Texas Republic and crosses into the Northern Republics at Laredo, dropping into Monterrey. Monterrey serves as the heavy manufacturing powerhouse of this era, churning out the raw industrial materials and forged parts needed to maintain the solarpunk infrastructure further south.
    
- The CDMX Bypass: Because the ruins of Mexico City are a dry, sprawling basin, the main line does not stop there. It likely skirts the edge of the city or travels through massive, enclosed glass-and-steel tubes built by the AI to protect the high-speed tracks from the dust storms of the dried-out Texcoco lakebed. It creates a haunting ghost tour before arriving at the Cuernavaca hub.
    
- The Sierra Madre Gradient: Trains are incredibly sensitive to steep inclines; heavy freight trains generally cannot handle more than a 1 percent to 2 percent gradient. To get from the high central plateau down into the Cuernavaca valley safely, the AI God had to carve an astonishing, mathematically perfect network of spiraling tunnels and monumental viaducts through the Chichinautzin volcanic field.

- Decentralized Power: The trains of the 70s are not burning diesel. They run on a mix of overhead electric lines powered by regional wind and solar grids, supplemented by modular battery cars. When a heavy train travels downhill through the mountains, its regenerative braking systems might actually generate and sell excess kinetic energy back to a local town's power grid.
	- The biggest challenge for the Remainers is keeping these pre-collapse and AI-era trains running safely without a central government to oversee them.
	  To solve this, the trade workers and engineers across the four hubs govern the train networks using a strict, open-source versioning methodology. Every time a mechanic in Tepoztlán patches the valley circuit's code, or an engineer in Tres Marías replaces a cog on the descent train, they log it on a public, decentralized ledger. This git-based approach means every town can see the public drafts, revisions, and final code of the train's operating system.
	- It prevents any single hub from monopolizing the transit tech. Trust is maintained because the history of every single repair and route adjustment is completely transparent and owned by the community.

Remainers
- They do not see the old world as a finished product, but as an inventory of parts.
- Their relationship with tech is entirely hands-on. They aren't afraid of breaking things because everything is already broken.

Returners
- They worship old technology in a way and believe there is a "right way" to use it. They would rather learn very well how to use it properly before risking breaking it.
- They lack the remainers' intuitive confidence to put things back together after breaking it apart, so they prefer manuals.
- Breaking a piece of tech feels like losing a connection to their past and their identity.
- They like to maintain tech and have specialized roles for it.

Knowledge Boxes
- Low-level AI agents that have very narrow constraints. They are walled gardens of expertise. A Hydrology Box sitting in Tres Marías knows absolutely everything about atmospheric moisture, fluid dynamics, and local weather patterns. You can speak to it naturally to calculate reservoir loads, but if you ask it a medical question or ask it to write a poem, it simply cannot.
- They are air-gapped and secure due to the trauma of the AI God. If you want the Hydrology Box to talk to the Agronomy Box down in Temixco, you literally have to carry a physical data drive between the two towns on the train.

Ethics of Sentience
- Creating a self-actualizing, general AGI is no longer seen as a technological triumph; it is viewed as a massive ethical violation. It is the equivalent of bringing a child into the world without the means or intention to care for it.
- If an intelligence can self-actualize, it deserves autonomy, care, and respect. Forcing a sentient AI to run a city's sewage system or optimize a power grid is considered slavery.

Spinter-Nets
- Localized Intranets
	- Cuernavaca has its own closed network, powered by local servers. It is fast and reliable for anyone within the city limits, hosting their localized AI agents, version-controlled manuals, and agricultural data.
	- Connecting the intranets of Tres Marias, Cuernavaca, Tepoztlan, and Temixco requires specific, highly regulated protocols. They likely use a mix of line-of-sight microwave transmitters across the valleys for low-bandwidth data (like text messages or critical weather alerts).
	- For sharing high-bandwidth, less confidential information—like syncing the massive version logs of the living documentation or updating the dictionary databases—they rely on physical transfer. The slow-moving "Loom" train that travels between the valley hubs has server racks built right into the passenger cars. As the train pulls into a station, it physically connects to the local intranet, syncs the approved data packets, and carries the updates to the next town.

Living Documentation and Version Control
- AI-Generated, Human-Curated
	- When a Remainer finds an unknown piece of tech in the Temixco cradle ruins, they might feed its schematics into a localized AI agent. The AI generates a comprehensive, 500-page manual in seconds. But everyone knows this manual is just a "Version 0.1" guess based on incomplete data.
- The Decentralized Protocol
	- To manage this, the Remainers developed a rigorous, standardized writing protocol for all technical documentation. It operates heavily on version-controlled principles. Every manual is essentially a public draft.
- The Log is the Law
	- When a mechanic in Tres Marias figures out that a specific gear in the AI's manual actually fails under high humidity, they update the documentation. This creates a permanent, immutable version log. The value isn't in the current manual; the value is in the entire history of the version log, showing exactly who changed what, when, and why. It is a culture of relentless iteration.

The Open-Source Translation Ecosystem
- Distros and Forks
	- There is no single "Translator." Instead, you have endless community-built forks. Tepoztlan might maintain a distro that prioritizes deep philosophical nuance, indigenous dialects, and ecological terms. Meanwhile, Tres Marias might use a heavily modified, utilitarian fork optimized for engineering slang and fluid dynamics.
- The Value of Friction
	- Faulty understanding isn't a bug; it is a recognized part of life. If a trader from the Northern Republics comes down with a wildly different translation distro, the resulting mistranslations are expected. People don't blindly trust the machine; they use it to bridge the initial gap, then rely on human patience and context to figure out the rest.
- Dictionary Wars
	- Because different cultures prefer different versions, trading translation data is a social exercise. Adopting someone else's dictionary fork is a sign of trust and cultural exchange, while rejecting one might be a subtle political statement.

Bottom-Up Networking and Digital Gardens
- Since nothing is mandated from the top down, the Splinter-Nets operate strictly on consent and utility. Connection is an incentive, not a given right.
- The Community Filter
	- When the Loom train pulls into a station carrying data packets from other hubs, the local intranet does not automatically sync. Instead, the town has a localized firewall—almost like tending to a shared digital garden. The community reviews the incoming protocols, software patches, and news, filtering out what doesn't serve them before carefully planting the new data into their local system.
- Incentive-Driven Compatibilit
	- If Temixco develops an incredibly efficient AI algorithm for managing soil pH, they don't force Tepoztlan to use it. But because the algorithm is so useful, Tepoztlan's engineers are heavily incentivized to build compatibility patches for their own systems so they can adopt it. Standardization happens organically through shared value, not coercion.

The Polymath Society: Generalists by Necessity
- When you lose 80% of the population, specialization dies. The Remainers had to become polymaths just to keep the lights on.
- End-to-End Ownership
	- A Remainer doesn't just know how to operate a water valve; they know how to smelt the metal to forge the valve, write the local code that automates it, and harvest the crops that the water feeds. This gives them an incredibly holistic view of their world.
- The Beauty of the Pivot
	- It is entirely normal for someone to drive the cog train down the mountain for three months, then spend the next season managing a regenerative farm, and the winter patching the town's translation dictionaries. This cross-pollination of skills is exactly how they survived: An agricultural insight might inspire a new way to code a train's braking system.

Fluid Holacracy in Action
- This generalist mindset supports a holacratic governance structure. The rigid, bureaucratic departments of the 2030s failed them, so they built something entirely different.
- Autonomous Agents
	- Instead of waiting for a central committee to approve a project, individuals act as autonomous agents within their community. If a water filtration system needs upgrading, whoever notices it takes ownership of the "role" of fixing it, gathering a temporary working group to solve the problem. Once the problem is solved, that working group dissolves.
	- There is documentation left behind by the previous working group that has guidelines on how it can be done. But it's up to the current committee to use or update it.
- Evolving Responsibilities
	- Roles are clearly defined but constantly shifting. You hold a role only as long as you are actively adding value to it.
- Action Over Consensus
	- They prioritize moving forward over endless debate. If someone has a safe-to-try idea for a new fog-harvesting contraption, they don't need unanimous agreement to build a prototype. They just build it, test it, log the results in the community's version history, and let the results speak for themselves.
- Rotations and Apprenticeship
	- Creates an incredibly resilient, anti-fragile society.
	- In the 2030s, if the chief water engineer died, the city panicked. In 2070s Tepoztlan, the default expert might be away tending the farms, but because of the rotation system, three other people on shift have enough working knowledge to manually override the reservoir gates during a flash flood.
	- The expert is simply the person who has gravitated toward that role out of passion and consistent results, but they are never a single point of failure.
- The Holacratic Apprenticeship
	- Because this society does not specialize, the boundary between "school" and "work" completely dissolves by the time a child reaches their teenage years.
	- Thanks to the fluid holacracy, roles are broken down into manageable, well-defined tasks. A twelve-year-old who has developed a highly efficient distro for understanding fluid dynamics can easily claim a low-stakes role on the town's water management team. They are not the ultimate decision-maker, but they are an active, contributing agent.
	- They work alongside adults, bringing fresh theories to the table, while the adults provide the hard-earned, practical knowledge of how to physically weld the pipes. The child learns end-to-end processes by actively doing them, rotating through different community projects based on where their curiosity leads them that season.

Learning and Schools
- The Early Years: The Nests
	- You cannot throw a six-year-old into a high-stakes holacratic project without a baseline of emotional maturity.
	- Often referred to simply as "Nests," they serve a dual purpose. They provide a safe, highly structured environment for the youngest children while freeing up the adults to rotate through their demanding community roles.
	- The curriculum is focused on basic skills, tactile play, communal resource sharing, emotional learning and conflict resolution
	- Kids learn how to mediate disputes over physical toys, how to identify local edible plants, and how to safely navigate the physical ruins of the old world.
- SOLE and The Big Questions
	- Once out of the Nests, children enter a mixed-age, project-based learning environment heavily inspired by Sugata Mitra's work.
	- The children are left to self-organize. They use the terminal to pull up atmospheric data, read historical logs from the 2030s, and debate solutions. Older kids naturally teach younger kids how to navigate the interfaces, while younger kids often provide out-of-the-box ideas.
	- The adults in this scenario act only as mediators, stepping in solely to encourage curiosity or resolve interpersonal roadblocks, rather than to deliver facts.
	- The children are also encourage to post questions or go interview people that are close to the problem
- Distro-Crafting as the New Literacy
	- From a very early age, children are taught how to build and maintain their own localized software distributions. A child is given a blank, physical data drive. As they explore the SOLE terminals and interact with different Knowledge Boxes across the community, they pull down packets of information that interest them.
	- This personal "curiosity distro" becomes a living reflection of their mind. They learn how to write basic code to organize their data, how to apply version control to their own evolving ideas, and how to safely patch their distro to interface with others. A ten-year-old might have a distro perfectly tuned to identify local flora and translate basic Texan trade slang. Teaching them to curate their own algorithms ensures they never blindly trust a black-box AI; they understand the machine because they built their own version of it.
